What 15 of the World's Biggest Investors Actually Think About Crypto

Bitwise interviewed endowments, pension funds, and sovereign wealth funds directly. None of them sold during a roughly 50% drawdown, and every single one still holds mostly Bitcoin.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 27, 2026 ·  6 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

Most of what gets published about institutional crypto adoption is inferred — ETF flow data, 13F filings, secondhand reporting. On September 23, Bitwise published something different: direct interviews with 15 of the world's largest institutional allocators — endowments, foundations, public pension funds, multi-family offices, investment consultants, sovereign wealth funds, and public companies — about how they actually think about the asset class, not just what their filings show.

Modest, and not moving

The headline allocation number is unglamorous: most institutions surveyed hold between 1% and 2% of investable assets in crypto, with a wider range across the group of 0.5% to 13%. Every single institution interviewed owns Bitcoin, and for all of them it's the largest and longest-held crypto position — increasingly paired with gold in the same allocation bucket, treated as a similar kind of hedge rather than a growth trade. Ethereum and Solana show up too, but in smaller positions, held for shorter time horizons, and with explicit conditions attached for when the institution would exit.

The drawdown nobody sold into

The most striking finding is behavioral. Crypto fell by roughly 50% between the fourth quarter of 2025 and the second quarter of 2026 — a real, painful drawdown by any standard. None of the institutions Bitwise interviewed reduced their allocations during it. Several increased their positions instead. When asked what would actually trigger a sale, the answers clustered around thesis failure, a regulatory reversal, or an industry-wide credibility crisis — not price. Not one institution cited the price drop itself as a reason to consider selling.

Bitwise's own framing of the finding is worth quoting directly: "The common assumption is that institutions are the weak hands in a crypto drawdown. These interviews suggest the opposite." The report also found an inverse relationship between allocation size and internal approval friction — family offices, which typically face the least internal bureaucracy, held the largest allocations in the sample, while sovereign wealth funds, which face the most committees and mandates, held the smallest.

Read the sample size honestly

Fifteen institutions is a real, direct-interview sample — a meaningfully different kind of evidence than survey aggregation — but it's still fifteen. This isn't proof that institutional capital broadly behaves this way; it's a data point that the largest, most sophisticated allocators willing to talk openly about their crypto exposure are, so far, not the panic sellers retail narratives sometimes assume they'd be. Whether that holds at a much larger scale, or in a drawdown that goes on longer than the one measured here, is still an open question.

Why it matters beyond the sample size caveat

The reason this report is worth taking seriously despite its small sample is who's in it: these aren't retail investors or crypto-native funds, they're the same institutions that set the tone for how conservative, mainstream capital treats a new asset class over the following decade. When the most risk-averse allocators in the room describe a 50% drawdown as noise rather than a reason to exit, that's a meaningfully different signal than a retail investor saying the same thing on social media — institutional mandates are written specifically to prevent exactly the kind of emotional, price-driven decision-making that didn't happen here. Whether that discipline holds through a longer or deeper downturn than the one Bitwise measured is the open question worth watching next. There's also a practical read-through for the family offices and pension consultants who haven't yet allocated anything: the inverse relationship between bureaucracy and allocation size suggests that the biggest obstacle to broader institutional adoption isn't conviction about the asset itself, but internal approval processes built for asset classes that behave nothing like crypto — a governance problem more than an investment-thesis problem. That distinction matters for how the next leg of institutional adoption actually plays out: it will likely come less from more allocators being convinced Bitcoin is a good investment, and more from existing skeptics finally streamlining the internal approval chain that's been the real bottleneck all along. If that's right, the next meaningful adoption signal to watch for isn't a bigger headline number from a bigger fund — it's smaller, boring-sounding changes to investment-committee charters and custody policies that quietly clear the path for money that was already willing to move. Those changes rarely make headlines on their own, which is exactly why they're worth watching closely rather than waiting for the next big allocation announcement to notice the shift has already happened.

Frequently asked

Does this mean institutions are about to pour much more money into crypto?

Not necessarily. The report shows conviction — institutions aren't selling — but most allocations are still capped around 1-2%, and the interviewees didn't describe plans for dramatically larger allocations in the near term.

Which crypto asset do institutions actually trust most?

Bitcoin, by a wide margin. Every institution interviewed held it as their largest and longest-held position, increasingly alongside gold. Ethereum and Solana appear in smaller, shorter-horizon positions with explicit exit conditions attached.

Why do family offices hold more crypto than sovereign wealth funds?

The report found allocation size inversely correlated with internal approval friction — family offices typically face fewer layers of committee approval than sovereign wealth funds, which tend to operate under more conservative mandates and more oversight before making or changing an allocation.
