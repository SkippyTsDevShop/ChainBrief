The SEC Just Let Real Stocks Trade on Public Blockchains

A five-year exemption lets tokenized U.S. shares trade on automated market makers on Ethereum, Solana, and BNB Chain without those venues registering as exchanges. Companies get 30 days' notice and a veto before their stock shows up.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 21, 2026 ·  7 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

On September 17, the SEC granted a five-year exemptive-relief framework that lets tokenized U.S. stocks trade directly on automated market makers (AMMs) — decentralized trading venues that price assets algorithmically instead of matching buy and sell orders the way a traditional exchange does — running on public blockchains including Ethereum, Solana, and BNB Chain. The venues don't have to register as securities exchanges to do it. This isn't the synthetic "stock token" model some platforms have offered for years, where a derivative tracks a share's price without conferring any actual ownership. The SEC's order requires real shares, carrying real dividend and voting rights, represented on-chain.

How the exemption actually works

Exemptive relief is a specific SEC tool: rather than writing a new rule through the full notice-and-comment process, the agency uses existing statutory authority to exempt a defined activity from requirements that would otherwise apply — in this case, exchange registration. It's the same general mechanism the SEC has used before to let novel financial products operate ahead of, or instead of, a full rulemaking (spot ETFs are a familiar example). That makes this framework flexible and fast to issue, but also less durable than a statute: a future SEC could narrow or revoke it without needing Congress.

The guardrails

The relief isn't unconditional. Trading venues have to notify a company 30 days before its shares begin trading on-chain, and the company can object and block participation — an issuer veto that doesn't exist for ordinary secondary-market trading of a stock today. Venues also have to run know-your-customer (KYC) checks, apply trading limits, and layer permissioned access controls on top of what are otherwise open, permissionless blockchains. In practice, that means the underlying settlement layer is public, but who can actually trade isn't — a hybrid that regulators clearly designed to keep AML and investor-protection obligations intact rather than exporting them to code.

Who's positioned to benefit — and who's shut out

Tokenization platforms including Securitize, Bullish, Superstate, Dinari, and Fairmint are positioned to build the compliant rails this framework requires. Coinbase and Robinhood, both of which already run brokerage infrastructure, are expected to have a head start adapting to it. DeFi trading venues like Uniswap, Aerodrome, and Raydium are the AMMs this relief was written for in the first place.

Notably excluded: the synthetic products that already exist. Robinhood's earlier Stock Tokens product and Kraken's xStocks track share prices without granting real ownership, dividends, or voting rights — exactly the model this exemption doesn't cover. Ondo's offshore tokenized-equity products fall outside it for similar reasons. If those platforms want to operate inside this new framework, they'd need to rebuild around real, custodied shares rather than price-tracking derivatives.

“This gives a way to trade real tokenized stocks,” Securitize CEO Carlos Domingo said, calling it a step that will “accelerate adoption of native tokenized security.”

Why this is the same story as the CLARITY Act, again

This is the fourth piece we've covered in the past week of regulators moving on their own after the Senate failed to pass the CLARITY Act: the SEC and CFTC's earlier pledge to act with or without Congress, Circle launching its own permissioned blockchain, and now an actual exemption letting real securities trade in DeFi. None of it required a single vote in the Senate. Whether that's a pragmatic way to keep U.S. markets competitive while Congress sorts itself out, or a workaround that leaves Wall Street's newest market structure resting on the discretion of whichever administration is in charge, is exactly the durability question we raised in our cloture explainer — and it applies here just as directly.

What to watch

The 30-day notice window is the first real test: does any major company actually exercise its veto and block its own shares from trading on-chain, and if so, why? Coinbase and Bullish shares both jumped on the news — Bullish reportedly around 10% and Securitize around 14% — which tells you the market is pricing this as a genuine new revenue line, not a symbolic gesture. Whether trading volume on the AMMs themselves follows is the harder, slower question.
