Real-World Asset Tokenization Just Crossed $34 Billion. Here's What That Actually Means.

Tokenized equities are up 390% this year, bonds and money markets make up more than half the market, and the whole category still represents about 0.01% of the assets it's tokenizing. Both of those facts are true at once.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 21, 2026 ·  7 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

The tokenized real-world asset (RWA) market — bonds, private credit, real estate, commodities, and now equities, all represented as tokens on a blockchain — reached $34.18 billion in mid-September 2026, up 85.2% since the start of the year. Tokenized equities specifically are up 390.4% year-to-date, the fastest-growing category by far, helped along by the SEC's new exemptive-relief framework letting real shares trade on public blockchains. Both of those numbers are real. So is a third one that gets left out of most of the celebratory coverage: even at $34 billion, tokenized assets represent roughly 0.01% of the estimated $300 trillion in traditional assets they're theoretically capable of representing.

Where the money actually is

Bonds and money-market instruments dominate the category, at $18.29 billion — 53.5% of the entire RWA market — though that's still only about 0.017% of the global bond and money-market universe. Tokenized equities have grown the fastest but remain the smallest major slice in absolute terms, at $4.43 billion against a $151.9 trillion global listed-equity market — 0.003% penetration. Private credit, real estate (up 17.9% year-to-date), and gold and commodities (up 46.6%) round out the rest, each tokenizing at meaningfully different rates depending on how illiquid and opaque the underlying asset class already is — which tracks the three-question utility test we've applied to tokenization claims before: tokens tend to do real work where an asset genuinely lacked liquid, verifiable infrastructure to begin with.

The gap between issuing a token and using one

The more useful number in this data isn't total value tokenized — it's how much of that value is actually doing anything. Across the RWA market broadly, only about 12% of tokenized capital is active on-chain in any meaningful sense — deployed into lending markets, liquidity pools, or collateral, rather than just sitting as a static token representing an off-chain asset. For tokenized equities specifically, that "capital activation rate" sits at 7.54%, with most of the active share concentrated in liquidity pools (65.4%) and lending protocols (28.1%). Put plainly: most tokenized assets right now are tokenized and then left alone, not actively used the way the technology's proponents describe.

AUM doesn't show how much capital is actually working — issuance and usage are two different numbers, and the gap between them is the real story in this data.

An early but telling pattern: derivatives

One data point worth watching: 58.5% of early tokenized-equity users are also interacting with perpetual futures on the same assets. That's a meaningfully different use case than "I want to own a fractional, on-chain share of a company" — it suggests a real chunk of current activity is traders using tokenized equities as one more instrument inside an already-active derivatives strategy, not new capital entering equity markets for the first time. Whether that shifts as the SEC's new framework matures, or stays a trading-desk phenomenon, is worth tracking over the next several quarters.

For deeper RWA coverage

Tokenization of real-world assets is a big enough beat that it has its own dedicated publication in our network — our sister site RWAToday.news covers the RWA sector in more depth and on an ongoing basis, if this is a category you're tracking closely.

What to watch

Growth in issuance is the easy number to celebrate and the least informative one on its own. The harder, more useful number is whether the capital-activation rate — the share of tokenized assets actually being used as collateral, in lending, or in liquidity provision — climbs meaningfully as more venues open up under frameworks like the SEC's new exemption. That's the number that tells you whether tokenization is building real financial infrastructure or mostly restating existing assets in a new wrapper.
