The Senate Votes on the CLARITY Act Tomorrow. It Should Pass.

Tuesday's 2:15 p.m. cloture vote isn't final passage — it's the last real test of whether Washington can give crypto the regulatory clarity it's been promised for a decade. Here's the case for clearing it, and what's actually standing in the way.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 14, 2026 ·  8 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

Editorial: this is a ChainBrief.news opinion piece, reflecting our own editorial judgment on pending legislation — not a straight news report, and not legal or financial advice. Our news coverage and our opinion pages are produced separately.

At 2:15 p.m. ET on Tuesday, the Senate holds a cloture vote on the motion to proceed to the CLARITY Act — formally the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act. It is not a vote on final passage. It's a procedural vote to end debate and let the Senate actually take up a bill that would, for the first time, give the United States a real statutory answer to a question that has hung over the crypto industry for a decade: which federal regulator is actually in charge. Clearing it takes 60 votes. Republicans hold 53 seats. They need at least seven Democrats to cross over, and as of this writing that math is not locked in.

We think the Senate should clear it. Not because the bill is perfect — it isn't, and we'll get to exactly where it falls short — but because the alternative on offer isn't a better bill. It's more of the same regulatory limbo that has already pushed companies, jobs, and enforcement clarity offshore, with no serious replacement plan waiting in the wings if this vote fails.

What tomorrow's vote actually is

As Senator Bernie Moreno (R-OH), one of the bill's floor advocates, put it ahead of the vote: this is “not a vote on final passage. It is a vote to end debate on whether the United States Senate should even consider a bill to regulate digital assets.” Clearing cloture opens the floor to amendments and, eventually, a final passage vote. If the Senate does pass its own version, it would still need to be reconciled with the market structure bill the House already passed in July 2025 before anything reaches the president's desk. Tomorrow is a gate, not a finish line — but it's the gate the bill has been stuck behind for months, after the Senate adjourned for its summer break in August without taking it up.

The case for a yes vote

The core problem the CLARITY Act is trying to solve is jurisdictional: for years, whether a given digital asset falls under the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) or the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has depended on shifting enforcement postures rather than a clear statutory line. The bill draws that line — giving the CFTC primary authority over digital commodity spot markets like Bitcoin and Ethereum, leaving the SEC with jurisdiction over tokens that function as investment contracts, and requiring joint rulemaking where the two overlap. It also sets out a measurable test for when a token has matured out of securities treatment, and imposes custody rules aimed directly at the kind of customer-fund commingling that has caused past platform collapses to drag ordinary users into bankruptcy proceedings to get their own money back.

That combination is why the bill's support isn't limited to the crypto industry anymore. The National Fraternal Order of Police and the National Sheriffs' Association, both previously opposed, dropped their objections after anti-money-laundering and illicit-finance provisions were added. And the current draft isn't a first pass rushed to the floor — supporters point to more than 120 substantive changes made at Democrats' request over roughly a year of negotiation, which is a meaningfully different posture than a party-line bill daring the other side to vote no.

Where it genuinely falls short

None of that means the roadblocks in front of this bill are manufactured or bad-faith, and a case for passage that pretended otherwise wouldn't be worth much. Four fights are real, and worth taking seriously on their own terms.

Ethics and the Trump family's crypto business. This has been the central sticking point. Democrats, led in negotiations by Senators Kirsten Gillibrand (D-NY) and Cory Booker (D-NJ), wanted an enforceable bar on senior federal officials — and by extension the president's family — profiting from crypto ventures while shaping crypto policy. This week the White House agreed to roughly 80% of a compromise proposal from Senators Thom Tillis (R-NC) and Ruben Gallego (D-AZ): certain officials would have to divest meaningful crypto holdings or place them in a blind trust, state attorneys general and the Justice Department would both gain authority to sue exchanges that list assets barred under the bill, and the restrictions would extend to federally elected officials, judges, and their spouses. Booker withdrew from the negotiations regardless, and Gillibrand has said she won't support the bill without an enforceable ban she considers sufficient — whether this draft clears that bar for her and for the handful of Democrats Republicans actually need is the single biggest open question heading into Tuesday.

State enforcement authority. A bipartisan coalition of 18 state attorneys general, led by New York's Letitia James and including both Republican and Democratic AGs, sent a letter opposing the bill over what they read as an ambiguous “qualified transaction” definition that could let defendants argue federal law preempts state securities and commodities enforcement. They point to FBI figures putting crypto fraud losses at $11.4 billion in a single year as the stakes of getting that carve-out wrong, and argue that recent language “reserving” state fraud authority is too vague to rely on. This is a legitimate drafting concern, not obstruction — preemption language is exactly the kind of thing that reads fine in a press release and falls apart in a courtroom if it isn't precise.

The stablecoin rewards fight. Banks — backed by the Independent Community Bankers of America, the American Bankers Association, and all 77 state banking associations — are pushing to tighten language around stablecoin “activity-based rewards,” arguing they function like deposit interest and would let crypto firms compete with banks without equivalent regulation. Crypto firms counter that a broad restriction would kill a legitimate product category outright. Neither side is being unreasonable; they simply want the line drawn in a different place.

DeFi's edges. The bill tries to exclude genuinely non-custodial developers and decentralized finance (DeFi) protocols from registration requirements built for centralized intermediaries, but where exactly that line sits — and what anti-money-laundering duties, if any, apply to protocols with no operator to regulate — remains unresolved and will likely need further rulemaking even if the bill passes as written.

“After 12 months of negotiations, it’s now or never for this strong, bipartisan compromise.” — Senator Cynthia Lummis (R-WY)

Who's actually pushing this

On the Republican side, Senate Banking Chair Tim Scott (R-SC) is leading the effort, with Lummis chairing the chamber's Digital Assets Subcommittee and John Boozman (R-AR) among the bill's other sponsors. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has called passage a “national security priority,” and the White House's crypto policy lead has said, simply, “I believe we'll get this done.” Gallego remains the clearest Democratic vote in favor after co-authoring the ethics compromise with Tillis, though Republicans need several more Democrats beyond him to actually clear 60.

Outside the Capitol, Stand With Crypto — the advocacy group backed by Coinbase, with a claimed network of roughly three million advocates — has spent August generating nearly 50,000 congressional contacts and running events and op-eds in states including Oklahoma, Kentucky, and Iowa, and has endorsed 32 lawmakers it considers pro-CLARITY Act. That grassroots push sits inside a much larger wave of crypto-linked political spending reported at upward of $190 million tied to this fight — a scale of spending that cuts both ways in how it should be read: it's a real signal of how much the industry wants this bill, and a real reason to keep scrutinizing whether the policy holds up on its own merits rather than the size of the campaign behind it.

What the prediction markets are saying

If you want a gut check on how contested this actually is, prediction markets are a reasonable place to look — with the caveat that these are real-money betting markets, not certainties, and the numbers move by the hour. As of Monday morning, Polymarket had roughly a 28-30% chance priced in on the CLARITY Act becoming law by the end of 2026, up sharply from around 12% two weeks earlier, with the jump tracking the Tillis-Gallego ethics deal. Kalshi's contract on broader crypto market-structure legislation passing by January 1, 2027 sat in a similar 24-26% range as of the most recent tracking. Both markets have swung hard in both directions over the past month — one tracker noted odds briefly sinking as low as 14% after a large single bet against passage, before recovering on the ethics news.

Separately, a Kalshi contract specifically on whether the Senate would hold a vote at all before October 1 sat above 90% — which is a useful distinction: the market is confident Tuesday's vote happens. It is far less confident the underlying bill actually becomes law this year. Read plainly, that's not a market calling this a done deal. It's a market saying the outcome is genuinely in play, which is precisely why the next several days of lobbying, and the actual roll call Tuesday, matter.

Why we're coming down in favor

None of the four roadblocks above are fake, and a handful of the senators and state officials raising them are doing exactly what they're supposed to be doing. But weighed against the cost of another year of the status quo — continued jurisdictional ambiguity, continued incentive for serious companies to build compliance infrastructure offshore instead of onshore, and continued reliance on case-by-case enforcement actions instead of a public statutory standard — we think the balance favors clearing cloture and letting the amendment process do the rest of the work these concerns deserve.

The ethics language on the table now is stronger than what was on offer even a month ago, not because anyone involved suddenly had a change of heart, but because Democratic negotiators extracted real concessions and got roughly 80% of what they asked for in writing. The state AGs' preemption concern is fixable in the amendment process that a successful cloture vote actually opens up — it is not fixable by leaving the bill stuck in procedural limbo indefinitely. And the stablecoin and DeFi fights are the kind of technical line-drawing that rulemaking and future legislative fixes are built to handle; they are not, on their own, reasons to sink a decade-overdue jurisdictional framework.

Tuesday's vote is a chance to move a genuinely negotiated, twice-revised, increasingly bipartisan bill out of procedural purgatory and into the daylight of an actual floor debate, where its remaining flaws can be argued over in public and amended on the record. That's a better outcome than another year of nothing. The Senate should vote yes on cloture.
