Circle Built Its Own Blockchain. The Gas Token Is USDC.

Arc went live on mainnet September 16 — one day after the Senate failed to pass crypto market structure legislation. Eleven of the world's largest financial institutions are already running its validators. It is not decentralized, and Circle isn't pretending otherwise.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 14, 2026 ·  7 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

On September 16, Circle — the company behind USDC, the second-largest dollar-pegged stablecoin — launched Arc, its own layer-1 (L1) blockchain, on mainnet. The timing is hard to read as coincidence: one day earlier, the Senate had failed to clear a cloture vote on the CLARITY Act, the bill that would have given digital assets a clear federal jurisdictional line. While Washington's attempt at a public framework stalled, one of the industry's largest stablecoin issuers quietly finished building a private one.

What makes Arc different

Arc's core design choice is that USDC itself is the network's native gas token — every transaction fee is paid, and denominated, in dollars, rather than a separate volatile network asset the way ETH or SOL function on Ethereum and Solana. Fee pricing is set by a weighted moving average of network demand rather than adjusting block to block, which is aimed squarely at giving institutional users a predictable cost rather than a speculative one.

Consensus runs on Malachite, a Byzantine fault-tolerant engine derived from Tendermint, which Circle says delivers deterministic finality in well under a second — a transaction is either final the moment its block closes, or it isn't, with none of the probabilistic "wait a few more confirmations to be safe" behavior that networks like Ethereum and Bitcoin rely on. The execution layer is built on Reth and is fully EVM-compatible, so existing Solidity contracts and standard Ethereum tooling deploy on Arc largely unmodified. It also ships with an optional privacy layer that can shield transfer amounts — a feature aimed directly at institutions that need to settle on a shared ledger without broadcasting position sizes to competitors.

Who's actually running it

This is the part that separates Arc from almost every other L1 launch: its validator set isn't open. Eleven founding validators — DTCC, ICE (parent of the NYSE), Mastercard, Visa, Worldpay, BlackRock, Galaxy, Apollo Funds, Standard Chartered, SBI Group, and Sumitomo — were selected directly by Circle, with a broader rollout of roughly twenty SOC 2-certified operators planned in phases. More than a hundred applications, including Aave, Morpho, and Uniswap-adjacent infrastructure, reportedly went live alongside the mainnet launch.

Circle raised $222 million in a May 2026 presale at a $3 billion fully diluted valuation, with backing from a16z crypto, ARK Invest, BlackRock, Apollo, ICE, SBI Group, and several other institutional investors — capital that reads less like a speculative token launch and more like a consortium of the exact institutions Arc is now asking to settle transactions on it.

Ethereum and Solana spent years arguing about how to decentralize without sacrificing speed. Arc skips the argument. It picks institutional throughput and a known validator set, and says so plainly.

The decentralization question nobody's dodging

It's worth naming the obvious tension directly, the same way we did in looking at what actually makes a blockchain "decentralized": Arc's validator set is permissioned, chosen by one company, dominated by some of the largest financial institutions on earth. That is a fundamentally different bet than Ethereum's or Solana's permissionless model, where anyone who meets the technical requirements can run a validator or a node. Circle isn't marketing Arc as a decentralization story — it's marketing it as an institutional settlement story, where a known, accountable validator set is treated as a feature, not a compromise. Whether that tradeoff is acceptable depends entirely on what you're using the chain for, and it's a materially different product than the crypto-native chains it runs alongside.

What it's actually for

The stated use cases are concrete rather than speculative. DTCC has said it plans to begin tokenizing DTC-custodied assets on Arc starting in 2027, covering repo transactions, collateral mobility, and corporate actions. BlackRock has signaled it will bring its tokenized Treasury fund onto the network. The pitch to these institutions isn't "decentralize your business" — it's "settle dollar-denominated transactions faster, with fewer intermediaries, on a ledger built by the company that already issues the dollar-equivalent token you're settling in."

Why the timing matters

Arc didn't need Congress to pass anything to launch. That's arguably the whole story here: while the CLARITY Act's failure leaves the SEC and CFTC's jurisdictional lines exactly as contested as they were a week ago, the institutions that need certainty to move real money on-chain aren't waiting for a statute — they're building the regulated, permissioned, compliance-forward version themselves, and asking regulators to work with what already exists rather than what Congress might eventually pass. Whether that's a pragmatic bridge to broader adoption or a way of building around public accountability entirely is a fair question, and one worth watching as Arc's institutional use cases actually go live in 2027.
