Aave, Morpho, and Uniswap Just Deployed on a Chain They Don't Control

More than a hundred applications went live on Circle's Arc the same day it launched — including some of DeFi's most established names. That's a genuine bet on a chain whose validator set they have no vote in.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 17, 2026 ·  6 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

When Circle's Arc blockchain went live on mainnet, it didn't launch quiet. More than a hundred applications reportedly deployed alongside it on day one, including an Aave V4 market, Morpho, and Uniswap-adjacent infrastructure, plus a dozen major exchanges routing order flow its way. That's notable for a reason that has nothing to do with transaction speed: Aave, Morpho, and Uniswap built their reputations on permissionless composability — code anyone can build on top of, with no company able to decide who's allowed to participate. Arc's validator set is chosen entirely by Circle.

What actually deployed

The specifics matter more than the headline count. Aave, one of DeFi's largest lending protocols, brought a V4 market to Arc. Morpho, a peer-to-peer lending protocol known for capital efficiency, did the same. Infrastructure adjacent to Uniswap, the largest decentralized exchange by volume, also went live. Alongside them, a dozen centralized exchanges — Binance, Kraken, OKX, Gate, and KuCoin among them — integrated support for the network almost immediately. That's a genuinely unusual amount of coordinated day-one liquidity for a brand-new L1.

Why protocols built for permissionless chains would show up here

The honest answer is that liquidity follows liquidity, and Arc launched with an unusually concentrated pool of it: eleven founding validators that include BlackRock, DTCC, and several of the largest payment networks on earth, several of which have said they intend to move real institutional assets — tokenized Treasuries, repo transactions — onto the chain starting in 2027. For a lending or trading protocol, that's a liquidity source worth being early to, regardless of who's running the validator set underneath it. There's also a genuine user-experience case: USDC as the native gas token means a user doesn't need to separately acquire and hold a volatile network token just to pay transaction fees, which removes a real point of friction for less crypto-native users.

DeFi spent years engineering around the need to trust anyone. Deploying on Arc means trusting, at minimum, that Circle’s validator selection stays fair — a dependency these protocols spent their whole design philosophy trying to avoid.

The tension nobody's pretending away

This isn't a subtle contradiction, and DeFi builders deploying on Arc aren't hiding from it — it's simply a different bet than the one their protocols were originally designed around. A permissionless chain means no single company can remove a builder from the network, no matter how unpopular that builder becomes. Arc's validator set, chosen and managed by Circle, doesn't offer that same guarantee. If Circle ever wanted to exclude an application, restrict which assets can be traded, or prioritize its own institutional partners over a smaller DeFi team, nothing about Arc's architecture structurally prevents it the way Ethereum's permissionless validator set does. That may never happen — but it's a real dependency that didn't exist for these protocols on their original chains.

Not without precedent, but a different order of scale

DeFi protocols deploying on a more centralized environment isn't new — plenty already run on corporate-backed layer-2 networks with their own operational dependencies. What's different about Arc is the identity and concentration of who's actually running it: not one company's infrastructure team, but a validator set stocked with the DTCC, the parent company of the NYSE, and two of the world's largest payment networks. That's a qualitatively different kind of institutional gatekeeping than a single L2 operator, and it's fair to treat DeFi's rush to build there as a bet that access to that liquidity is worth the tradeoff.

What to actually watch

The number that will actually answer whether this bet paid off isn't how many applications launched on day one — it's whether meaningful, sustained transaction volume and total value locked follow in the months after, or whether Arc ends up as a satellite deployment most of these protocols treat as optional. Institutional assets moving onto the chain in 2027, as DTCC and BlackRock have signaled, would be the clearer test of whether Arc becomes a genuine second home for DeFi liquidity or stays a launch-day headline.
