Five Utility Tokens, Put to the Test

"Utility token" roundups usually work backward from a token to a justification. We ran five well-known projects through the same three-question test we've argued should apply to any tokenization claim — and the results weren't uniform.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 14, 2026 ·  8 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

Round-ups of "utility tokens to watch" are a genre unto themselves, and most of them share the same structural flaw: they pick projects with a working product, then work backward to explain why the token attached to it is meaningful. We wanted to try the opposite. In an earlier piece on tokenization, we proposed a three-question test for cutting through the hype: does the token need multiple parties who don't fully trust each other to share a verifiable record, rather than one company's database? Does removing the token break the actual product, or just the price chart? And is the underlying process genuinely illiquid or opaque today in a way a token fixes? Here's five tokens run through that test, plainly, with no attempt to make all five come out looking the same.

Chainlink (LINK) — passes

Chainlink's oracle network feeds external, real-world data — prices, weather, sports results, proof of reserves — onto blockchains that otherwise have no native way to know anything happening off-chain. The token secures the network: node operators stake LINK as collateral they lose if they report bad data, which is a genuinely different trust model than a single company's data feed. Remove the token, and the economic incentive that makes operators accountable for accuracy disappears with it — this isn't a database with a price chart bolted on; the token is doing real work, and a large share of on-chain DeFi activity depends on oracle data actually being reliable.

Filecoin (FIL) — partial

Filecoin pays independent storage providers to store data and cryptographically prove, on an ongoing basis, that they're still storing it — a genuinely hard coordination problem across parties who don't trust each other by default. That part clears the bar. Where it gets murkier is that centralized cloud storage already exists, is cheaper in many cases, and is plenty transparent for buyers who simply want a service-level agreement rather than a cryptographic proof; the token adds real value specifically for use cases where censorship-resistance or provenance verification matters more than raw cost, which is a real but narrower slice of total storage demand than a bullish pitch usually implies.

Helium (HNT / MOBILE / IOT) — partial

Helium's pitch is that ordinary people can run wireless hotspots and get paid in tokens for providing coverage, building out a network no single carrier had to capitalize upfront — a genuine multi-party coordination problem a centralized telecom doesn't face in the same way. The honest caveat is that network usage and token issuance are two different curves, and the model only holds up long-term if actual paying data usage — not just hotspot deployment for reward-farming — keeps growing faster than token emissions. It's a real use case with a real dependency on demand catching up to supply, not a settled success story yet.

Render (RENDER) — partial

Render connects people who need GPU compute for 3D rendering and AI workloads with people who have idle GPU capacity to sell, paid in tokens. The coordination problem is real — matching distributed, unaffiliated compute supply with demand is exactly the kind of thing a token-based marketplace can do that a single company's rental fleet can't scale as elastically. The open question is competitive, not structural: centralized cloud GPU providers are also racing to expand capacity, and Render's advantage narrows or widens depending on how much idle, decentralized GPU supply actually exists relative to demand at any given time — a genuinely open market question, not a fixed answer.

Livepeer (LPT) — partial

Livepeer pays a distributed network of node operators to transcode video — converting a single video stream into the multiple formats and bitrates different devices need — for a fraction of what centralized cloud transcoding costs. The multi-party coordination case is genuine: no single company has to own all the transcoding hardware. What keeps this from a clean pass is that video transcoding is a commodity service where price and reliability, not decentralization, are what most customers actually optimize for — the token's advantage has to keep winning on cost and uptime against well-capitalized centralized competitors, not on ideology.

Not one of these five is a database with a price chart bolted on for no reason. But only one of them clears the test cleanly. The other four have a genuine multi-party coordination problem at their core — and a genuine, ongoing dependency on demand actually materializing at the scale their token models assume.

The pattern worth noticing

What separates these five from the token-for-token's-sake products we've written about before isn't that they're perfect — it's that every one of them is solving a real coordination problem between parties who don't fully trust each other, rather than dressing up something a single company's database already handled fine. That clears the first and most important bar. Whether each one's specific token-economic model actually holds up as demand scales is a separate, harder question, and one that deserves more scrutiny than most "tokens to watch" lists give it. This is not investment advice, and nothing here is a claim about where any of these tokens' prices are headed — it's an attempt to separate genuine utility from utility-shaped marketing, which is a different exercise entirely.
