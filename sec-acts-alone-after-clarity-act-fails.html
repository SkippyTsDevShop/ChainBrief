The CLARITY Act Failed. The SEC and CFTC Say They'll Regulate Crypto Anyway.

Two days after the Senate couldn't find 60 votes for a statutory jurisdictional line, both agencies said, in almost the same words, that they'd draw one themselves. That's faster than waiting on Congress — and far less permanent.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 17, 2026 ·  7 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

On September 15, the Senate's cloture vote on the CLARITY Act failed, 49-50 — short of both the 60-vote threshold and, for the first time in this fight, even a simple majority. Within 48 hours, the heads of both agencies whose turf the bill was supposed to settle said, in effect: fine, we'll draw the lines ourselves.

What Atkins said

SEC Chair Paul Atkins said the agency would proceed "with or without legislation," adding that the SEC will "act decisively within the SEC's statutory authority" to deliver certainty for investors and entrepreneurs. That's not a new posture in isolation — the SEC had already, in August, floated a tailored framework for certain investment contracts involving crypto assets — but saying it explicitly in the same week Congress failed to act reframes it as a substitute for legislation rather than a complement to it.

The agency's independent plans have some specificity to them. A proposed "Regulation Crypto Assets" framework reportedly includes a startup exemption pathway, a separate fundraising exemption, and a safe harbor for certain crypto investment contracts. Separately, the SEC and CFTC have jointly issued an interpretation establishing five digital token categories, four of which are treated as non-securities — effectively the jurisdictional taxonomy CLARITY would have written into statute, now being built through interpretive guidance instead.

What the CFTC said

CFTC Chair Michael Selig called the vote's outcome "unfortunate" and said Americans "deserve regulatory clarity, legal certainty, and consumer protections" in crypto markets regardless of how the Senate vote went. He described the agency as "locked in and ready to ship" its own rules, pointing to earlier direction given to CFTC staff to explore frameworks under the Commodity Exchange Act covering leveraged trading and a potential new "asset market" registration category for exchanges.

“We remain the crypto capital of the world.” — CFTC Chair Michael Selig, on the agency’s plan to proceed with rulemaking regardless of the CLARITY Act’s status

Faster isn't the same as durable

Agency rulemaking has one real advantage over waiting on Congress: it doesn't need 60 votes, or even 51. A commission can propose a rule, run a public comment period, and finalize it without a single legislative vote. That's precisely why both chairs are leaning on it now that the legislative path has stalled.

It comes with a real cost, though, and it's worth stating plainly rather than treating agency action as a clean substitute for a statute. Rules built on existing statutory authority are more vulnerable to legal challenge than a law Congress actually passed — courts have shown real willingness in recent years to strike down ambitious agency rulemaking under the major questions doctrine when the underlying statute doesn't clearly authorize it. And a rule written by one commission can be undone by the next one, in a way a statute generally can't be undone without another act of Congress. What the SEC and CFTC are offering now is real, but it's provisional in a way CLARITY, had it passed, would not have been.

What actually changes, and on what timeline

Nothing changes for market participants overnight. Each of these proposals still has to go through a commission vote to open formal rulemaking, a public comment period, and a final rule — a process that realistically runs months to years, not weeks, and can be reshaped or abandoned along the way. The practical upshot for now is a continuation of the same case-by-case regulatory posture the industry has operated under for years, just with both agencies now on the record saying they intend to move regardless of what Congress does next.
