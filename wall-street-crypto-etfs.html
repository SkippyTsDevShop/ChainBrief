Why Wall Street Keeps Launching Crypto Exchange-Traded Funds

Asset managers keep filing for new crypto exchange-traded funds even in a crowded field. The reason has less to do with conviction about crypto and more to do with how the asset management business actually makes money.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 14, 2026 ·  6 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

Once regulators in major markets began approving spot crypto exchange-traded funds — ETFs, meaning funds that trade on a stock exchange like a share of stock but hold an underlying asset, in this case crypto, directly — asset managers didn't file one or two and wait to see how it went. They kept filing. New wrappers, new baskets, new structures, from firms that spent years publicly skeptical of crypto as an asset class. That pattern is less about a sudden conversion to crypto conviction and more about how the fund business actually works.

The business logic is simple

Asset managers make money on fees collected as a percentage of assets under management. A new ETF category with real, sustained investor demand — the kind spot Bitcoin and Ether funds demonstrated — is a rare thing in an industry where most new fund launches attract limited assets. Once one firm proves a category works, competitors face a straightforward choice: launch a competing product and fight for a share of that fee pool, or cede the category entirely to whoever got there first. Most large firms chose the former, which is the simplest explanation for why the field kept getting crowded even as the flagship products matured.

What a crypto ETF actually replaces

The pitch to a traditional investor is straightforward: exposure to a crypto asset's price through a brokerage account they already have, with no need to set up a separate crypto exchange account, manage a digital wallet, or safeguard a private key — the cryptographic credential that controls access to crypto held directly. For an investor who's decided they want price exposure and nothing else, that's a meaningful reduction in operational complexity and a set of self-custody risks removed entirely.

What it doesn't replicate is anything that requires actually holding the underlying asset on-chain. An ETF holder generally isn't eligible for staking rewards — yield earned by participating in a proof-of-stake network's validation process — and has no ability to use the asset directly in decentralized applications, vote in on-chain governance, or move it peer-to-peer. It's also only tradable when the exchange it's listed on is open, while the underlying crypto asset itself trades continuously. Investors weighing a crypto ETF against direct ownership are really deciding how much of that on-chain functionality they actually plan to use.

The next wave of filings

Momentum in the category hasn't stopped at single-asset spot funds. Asset managers have filed for multi-asset baskets, funds structured to pass through staking rewards, and wrappers built around assets well outside Bitcoin and Ether. Each filing faces its own regulatory review and there's no guarantee of approval — the pace and shape of that pipeline is a genuinely open regulatory question, not a foregone conclusion, and it varies by jurisdiction.

An ETF doesn’t make an asset less volatile. It makes the asset easier to buy inside an account structure people already trust. That’s a distribution unlock, not a risk reduction — the two get conflated constantly.

What the trend actually signals

The steady drumbeat of new filings is best read as a distribution story, not a verdict on crypto's value. It means large asset managers now see durable client demand for regulated crypto exposure worth building a product around — that's a real signal about institutional acceptance. It says nothing about where any specific asset's price is headed, and it doesn't reduce the underlying volatility that crypto assets carry.
