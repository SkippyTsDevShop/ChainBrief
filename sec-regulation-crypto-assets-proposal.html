The SEC's Quiet End-Run Around a Stalled Congress

While the CLARITY Act sat dead in the Senate, the SEC proposed its own crypto capital-raising framework using authority it already has — no new statute required.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 27, 2026 ·  6 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

On August 18, the SEC proposed a rule it's calling Regulation Crypto Assets — and the timing tells you most of what you need to know about it. The Senate had already failed to pass the CLARITY Act's cloture vote, and rather than wait for Congress to try again, the SEC built a crypto-specific framework out of authority it already holds under the existing Securities Act.

The proposal doesn't touch trading venues or custody standards — that's left to the CFTC or a future rulemaking. What it does cover is capital formation: how a crypto project can legally raise money from U.S. investors without running the multi-year, multi-million-dollar gauntlet of a full securities registration.

Two new ways to raise money

The rule creates two registration exemptions sized for very different companies. One allows up to $5 million in token offerings over a four-year window — small-scale, closer to a Reg Crowdfunding tier. The other permits up to $75 million per 12-month period, in the range of Reg A+ offerings that growth-stage startups use today. Issuers using either exemption still owe investors principles-based narrative disclosures, and larger offerings under the $75 million tier owe actual financial statements and ongoing reporting — this isn't a disclosure-free pass, just a lighter one.

The more structurally important piece is a conditional safe harbor: under specific conditions, a crypto asset can be deemed outside the "investment contract" definition that has driven years of SEC enforcement actions against token issuers. Combined with a preemption clause waiving state securities registration requirements for qualifying offerings and certain secondary transactions, the practical effect is a project that follows the safe harbor's conditions doesn't have to fight fifty separate state regulators on top of the SEC.

"Onshore innovation"

SEC Chairman Paul Atkins framed the proposal as giving "crypto asset entrepreneurs and market participants clear pathways to raise capital," while preserving investor protections, and described it as part of a broader strategy to "onshore innovation in crypto asset markets" — regulatory shorthand for keeping token projects incorporating and building in the U.S. rather than Switzerland, Singapore, or the UAE. It's the same competitive framing that's shown up in the CFTC's own market-structure proposal, sent to the White House for review the same month Congress stalled.

What it doesn't settle

A 60-day public comment period runs from Federal Register publication, and comments will likely focus on how the safe harbor's conditions get defined — a badly drafted test could either fail to protect issuers who need it or accidentally shield genuine securities fraud. And because the proposal is silent on trading venues and custody, it leaves the exchange-level and custodial questions — arguably the ones that matter most for everyday investors — for a separate rulemaking, or for Congress to eventually settle if the CLARITY Act ever gets a second vote.

Why it matters for founders, investors, and the states

For a crypto founder, the practical effect is a materially cheaper legal path to raise money in the U.S. — the difference between needing a securities lawyer for a multi-year registration process and needing one for a lighter, faster exemption filing. For investors, the disclosures required even under the exemptions mean an offering built this way still has to explain what it is and what the money is for, just with less paperwork than a full IPO-style registration. The state preemption piece is the one worth watching most closely: state securities regulators, who currently run their own enforcement actions against token issuers independent of the SEC, would lose that authority over qualifying offerings. Several state regulators have pushed back hard on federal preemption in past crypto rulemakings, and this proposal is likely to draw the same fight during the comment period. Whichever way that fight goes, it will set a template other agencies watch closely: if the SEC can make a workable exemption stick through notice-and-comment rulemaking without new legislation, that's a signal to other regulators — banking, commodities, tax — that they don't need to wait on a gridlocked Congress to write crypto-specific rules of their own. For founders weighing timing, the practical calculus shifts too: a faster, cheaper exemption path lowers the cost of raising early, which could pull more token launches into a formal, disclosed process rather than the offshore or informal structures many projects have defaulted to while waiting on Congress.

Frequently asked

Does this mean crypto tokens are no longer securities?

No. It creates a conditional safe harbor where certain tokens can be treated as outside the "investment contract" test if specific conditions are met — it doesn't blanket-exempt crypto assets from securities law.

How is this different from the CLARITY Act?

The CLARITY Act would be a new law passed by Congress establishing SEC and CFTC jurisdiction over crypto markets. This is the SEC using rulemaking authority it already has under the existing Securities Act — it doesn't require Congress, and a future SEC could revise or replace it without a new statute.

Can a project still get sued even if it follows the safe harbor?

Possibly, depending on how the final conditions are written. The comment period will likely shape how airtight the safe harbor's conditions end up being — a loosely defined test could still leave issuers exposed if regulators later argue the conditions weren't actually met.
