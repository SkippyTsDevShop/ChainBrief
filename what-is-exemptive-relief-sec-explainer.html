What Is "Exemptive Relief," and Why Is the SEC Using It Instead of Writing New Rules?

The SEC didn't pass a law or even finish a formal rulemaking to let tokenized stocks trade on public blockchains. It granted an exemption — a decades-old tool, and a faster one, with a real tradeoff attached.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 21, 2026 ·  5 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

When the SEC let tokenized U.S. stocks trade on public blockchains this month, it didn't do it by passing a law, and it didn't do it through a full formal rulemaking either. It used something called exemptive relief — a tool that's been part of securities regulation for decades, and one that's showing up constantly in crypto policy coverage right now without much explanation of what it actually is.

The basic idea

Securities law bars certain activities by default unless an exception applies. Exemptive relief is the SEC using authority Congress already gave it — typically under specific provisions of the securities and investment-company statutes — to grant an exception for a defined activity, usually with conditions attached. It's not the agency ignoring the law; it's the agency using a tool the law explicitly built in for situations that don't fit neatly into existing categories.

How it's different from a full rulemaking

A formal SEC rule normally goes through notice-and-comment rulemaking: the agency proposes a rule, the public gets a window to submit comments, the agency responds to those comments, and only then does a final rule take effect. That process is designed to be thorough, and it's also slow — often taking many months or longer for anything contentious. Exemptive relief, by contrast, can be granted directly by the Commission for a specific applicant or a defined class of activity, without the same extended public process. It trades some of that deliberative rigor for speed.

Where this tool has been used before

This isn't new. Some of the SEC's more consequential market structure has actually run on exemptive relief rather than dedicated legislation — exchange-traded funds themselves operated for years under a patchwork of individual exemptive orders before the SEC eventually adopted a generalized rule covering most ETFs. The pattern is familiar: a new product type doesn't fit the existing rulebook, the SEC grants exemptions to let it operate while it studies the space, and a more permanent rule sometimes follows years later — sometimes it doesn't, and the exemption regime just continues.

The tradeoff nobody should skip past

Speed comes at a cost: durability. A statute passed by Congress is comparatively hard to unwind — it takes another act of Congress. An exemptive order is a Commission-level decision, which means a differently composed Commission, under a different administration, can revisit it. That's precisely the distinction SEC Commissioner Paul Atkins raised when he called for "congressional action to provide more durable clarity" even as the agency's own interpretive and exemptive tools do real work in the meantime.

Why it's everywhere in crypto coverage right now

With the CLARITY Act stalled in the Senate, exemptive relief and similar tools have become the primary way the SEC and CFTC are moving on crypto market structure at all. It's the mechanism behind this month's tokenized-stock trading framework, and it's part of the same broader pattern we've covered in the CFTC sending its own rulebook to the White House for review. None of it requires a single additional vote in the Senate — which is exactly why it's moving faster than legislation, and exactly why it's less permanent than legislation would be.

Frequently asked

Is exemptive relief the same as a new law?

No. Exemptive relief is granted by the SEC under its existing statutory authority to exempt a specific activity from a requirement that would otherwise apply. It doesn't create new law, and a future SEC can narrow or revoke it without Congress acting.

Has the SEC used exemptive relief for crypto before?

Yes, in a more limited form — some early crypto-adjacent products and services have relied on no-action letters or narrower exemptions. The September 2026 tokenized-stock framework is a larger, more structural use of the tool.

Why not just write a formal rule instead?

Formal rulemaking requires a notice-and-comment process that typically takes months to years. Exemptive relief can be granted much faster, which matters when an agency is trying to respond to market developments in real time rather than waiting on a multi-year process — or on Congress.
