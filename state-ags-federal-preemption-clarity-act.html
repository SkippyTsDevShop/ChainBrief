Why 18 State Attorneys General — Republicans and Democrats Alike — Wanted CLARITY Dead

Buried under the ethics fight and the stablecoin lobbying was a quieter, more technical objection that cut across party lines entirely: a bipartisan coalition of state attorneys general warned the bill would let crypto fraud defendants argue their way out of state court.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 17, 2026 ·  6 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

Most of the coverage of the CLARITY Act's failure focused on the ethics fight over the Trump family's crypto business, or the stablecoin-rewards dispute between banks and crypto firms. A quieter objection got less national attention despite coming from an unusually bipartisan coalition: eighteen state attorneys general, led by New York's Letitia James and including Republican Kris Kobach and Democrat Rob Bonta among the signatories, sent the Senate a letter urging it to reject the bill outright over federal preemption.

The core claim: "qualified transaction"

The AGs' objection centers on a specific phrase in the bill's text: its definition of a "qualified transaction." Their letter argues that definition would let the SEC's federal framework preempt state securities and commodities enforcement over transactions that would otherwise fall under state authority — meaning a state attorney general trying to bring a fraud case against a crypto platform could find a defendant arguing that federal law already occupies that ground, regardless of whether the SEC actually brought its own case.

The "reserved" language fig leaf

More recent drafts of the bill added language the AGs describe as "reserving" certain state fraud-prosecution powers — an attempt to address exactly this concern. The coalition's response was that the reservation language is too vague to rely on in practice: as the letter put it, they "remain firmly opposed to any federal statutory changes that would displace states' authority to oversee the securities and commodities markets," arguing that ambiguous statutory language is precisely the kind of thing that reads fine in a press release and collapses the first time a well-resourced defendant's legal team tests it in court.

“We remain firmly opposed to any federal statutory changes that would displace states' authority to oversee the securities and commodities markets.” — from the eighteen state attorneys general’s letter to the Senate

The stakes they cited

The letter leaned on FBI figures putting crypto-related fraud losses at $11.4 billion in a single year to argue for why the preemption question isn't academic. State attorneys general have historically been among the more active enforcers against crypto fraud targeting their own residents, often moving faster than a federal agency juggling a national caseload — losing that tool, even partially, is the concrete harm the coalition says it's trying to prevent.

Why the bipartisan makeup actually matters

Most of the CLARITY Act's fault lines split cleanly along industry-versus-critic or party lines. This one didn't. Attorneys general don't agree on much when it comes to crypto policy broadly, but protecting their own office's enforcement authority against federal preemption is a structural, institutional interest that cuts across party — a Republican AG in one state and a Democratic AG in another both lose the same tool if the preemption language is written badly, regardless of their party's general posture on crypto regulation.

What happens to this concern now

The CLARITY Act's stalled status means the preemption fight is paused, not resolved — the underlying drafting concern doesn't go away just because the bill isn't moving. It's also the kind of narrow, technical fix that's genuinely well-suited to the amendment process a successful cloture vote would have opened, rather than a reason to sink the entire bill on its own. If CLARITY or something like it comes back, expect the "qualified transaction" language to be one of the first things renegotiated — it's a fixable problem, not an irreconcilable one, and the eighteen AGs who signed this letter have already told the Senate exactly what a fix would need to look like.
