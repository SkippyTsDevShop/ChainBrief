The Race for a Federal Crypto Bank Charter Is Quietly Ending

Circle, Ripple, Paxos, BitGo, Fidelity Digital Assets, and now Crypto.com — a running list of crypto companies has spent 2026 turning OCC approval into the industry's new baseline for legitimacy.

By ChainBrief Staff ·  September 27, 2026 ·  7 min read
Share on X Share on LinkedIn

Eighteen firms applied to the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency for a national trust bank charter in 2025. More than half a dozen have now cleared at least conditional approval, and the highest-profile one, Circle, has already crossed the finish line — a pattern that's turned what used to be a niche regulatory filing into 2026's clearest signal of who the federal government considers a legitimate crypto custodian.

What a trust charter actually gets you

A national trust bank charter is a specific, limited thing: it lets a company custody assets under federal oversight, but it does not let that company take deposits or make loans like a commercial bank. For Crypto.com, which received conditional approval in February 2026 roughly four months after applying, the charter authorizes custody and staking of digital assets and the handling of trade settlement — the CEO called it "a major step closer to meeting leading institutions' needs for a qualified custodian under federal oversight."

Circle went further. On July 10, the company received final OCC approval to establish Circle National Trust, making it one of the first crypto-native firms to hold a completed national trust charter rather than a conditional one. The charter lets Circle offer fiduciary digital asset custody to institutional clients — banks, asset managers, other financial institutions — and sets up a path for USDC's own reserves to eventually be managed under the same federal trust structure. CEO Jeremy Allaire put it plainly: "Federal oversight of our trust bank sets a new standard for transparency, governance, and scale for Circle's infrastructure," adding that it "unlocks a new phase of adoption, where leading financial institutions can build on public blockchains with clarity and confidence."

Who else is in the queue

Circle, Ripple, and Paxos received conditional approvals in December 2025, alongside custody specialists BitGo and Fidelity Digital Assets. Bridge followed in early February 2026. One applicant, Erebor Bank, was approved for a full national bank charter rather than a trust charter — a materially different, and harder to get, designation that does permit deposits and lending. Mercury, Bunq, Nubank, World Liberty Financial, and Laser Digital are all still working through the pipeline.

Why this is a market-structure story, not just a compliance story

A federal trust charter answers a question institutional allocators have been asking for years: who is actually allowed to hold this on our behalf under the same regulatory umbrella as the rest of our custody relationships? Every major custody bank already answers to the OCC. Until this year, no crypto-native firm did. That's part of why this list of approvals reads alongside — not separately from — stories like the Fed's new stablecoin capital rules and staking finally showing up inside a crypto ETF: all three are pieces of the same shift, where the infrastructure institutions were already comfortable with elsewhere in finance is finally available for crypto, too.

Why it matters for anyone whose advisor is still on the sidelines

A lot of financial advisors and institutional allocators have sat out direct crypto custody for a straightforward reason: their own compliance departments couldn't sign off on a custodian that wasn't answerable to a federal banking regulator the way every other custody relationship in their business is. A completed trust charter removes that specific objection. It doesn't make crypto less volatile or turn a token into a safer asset, but it does mean the operational and regulatory argument against holding it through a qualified custodian is getting weaker month by month — which is exactly why this list of approvals is worth tracking even for readers who will never touch a trust bank charter document themselves. It also reshapes who ends up holding crypto custody market share long term: a state-licensed trust company can lose that license or face inconsistent treatment crossing state lines, while a federally chartered one operates under one uniform standard nationwide — an advantage that compounds as more advisors and institutions start shopping for a custodian by regulatory pedigree rather than brand name alone. Watch the next wave of applicants closely: once one or two firms prove the OCC charter process is workable end to end, competitors that sat out the first round tend to move quickly rather than cede the compliance high ground permanently. And for readers who are advisors themselves, this is worth raising proactively with clients rather than waiting to be asked — being able to point to a federally chartered custodian is a concrete answer to a compliance objection that, until recently, didn't have one.

Frequently asked

Can a company with a trust charter take my deposit like a bank?

No. A national trust bank charter permits custody of assets — holding them safely on a client's behalf — but not taking deposits or making loans. That's reserved for a full national bank charter, a separate and harder designation.

Is Circle's stablecoin USDC now backed by the federal government?

No. The charter lets Circle manage things like USDC's reserves under federal trust-bank oversight, which adds regulatory scrutiny and governance standards — it doesn't make USDC a government-backed or FDIC-insured product.

Why do so many crypto firms want this specific charter instead of a state license?

A single federal charter lets a firm operate nationally under one regulator, instead of separately licensing state by state — a meaningfully lower compliance burden for a company serving customers across the whole country.
